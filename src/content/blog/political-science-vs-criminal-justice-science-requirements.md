Students deciding between political science and criminal justice usually ask about the two things that show up in the course catalog last: how much science is required, and whether any of it involves a lab. The honest answer is that the choice between these two majors is almost never what determines your science load. Three other things do — and once you can see them, the catalog stops being confusing.

Requirements vary by institution, so treat everything below as the shape of the answer, not the answer itself. Your school's catalog for your catalog year is the only authority.

## The distinction that matters most: general education vs. major requirements

Nearly every science course a political science or criminal justice student takes comes from **general education**, not from the major. The major is a list of political science or criminal justice courses. General education is a separate list that every student at the institution completes regardless of major, and it is where the natural science requirement lives.

This matters because it means switching from political science to criminal justice usually does not change your science requirement at all. Both are social science majors housed in a college of arts and sciences (or, for criminal justice, sometimes a professional school), and both typically inherit the same institutional science core.

A common general education science requirement looks like this:

- Two natural science courses, roughly 6–8 credits
- At least one of them must include a laboratory component
- Choices drawn from biology, chemistry, physics, astronomy, geology, environmental science, or a physical science survey course for non-majors

Note what is usually *excluded*: psychology, sociology, economics, anthropology, and political science itself. They are social sciences. At most institutions they satisfy a different category, and a criminology or forensic-adjacent social science course will not fill a natural-science-with-lab slot no matter how scientific it sounds.

## What political science typically requires

Political science is usually awarded as a Bachelor of Arts, though some schools offer a BS track. The major itself is generally built from:

- An introductory survey, often American government
- Distribution across subfields: American politics, comparative politics, international relations, political theory
- A research methods course, and frequently a statistics course
- Upper-division electives and sometimes a capstone or senior seminar

There is normally **no natural science requirement inside the political science major**. The science you take is the general education science. What political science does often require is quantitative work — methods and statistics — which satisfies a quantitative reasoning requirement, not a lab science requirement. Do not assume one covers the other; at most schools they are separate boxes.

BA degrees frequently carry a foreign language requirement instead of extra science. That trade — more language, less science — is the most common structural difference between a BA and a BS, and it is worth knowing before you pick.

## What criminal justice typically requires

Criminal justice programs are awarded as a BA or a BS depending on the school, and occasionally as both. The core generally includes:

- Introduction to criminal justice or the criminal justice system
- Policing, courts, and corrections
- Criminology or criminological theory
- Criminal law and sometimes constitutional law or procedure
- Research methods and statistics
- Electives, an internship, or a field placement

Like political science, a standard criminal justice major usually has **no natural science requirement of its own**. A student in the general track ends up with the same one or two general education science courses a political science student takes.

Where criminal justice diverges is in what it *offers*. Many programs include a survey course such as introduction to forensic science or criminalistics. These are sometimes listed with a lab section and sometimes not, and at some schools the survey course counts as a natural science for general education purposes while at others it explicitly does not. This is one of the most common places students get the requirement wrong in both directions — taking a lab they did not need, or assuming a course counted when it did not.

## The real fork: forensic science concentrations

If there is a genuine science divide between these two paths, it is not political science versus criminal justice. It is criminal justice versus **forensic science**, which is often marketed as a concentration inside a criminal justice department but is a fundamentally different curriculum.

A forensic-track or forensic science degree commonly requires a science core that looks like a pre-med schedule:

- General chemistry I and II with labs
- Organic chemistry, sometimes with labs
- General biology with lab, often molecular or cell biology
- Frequently physics with lab, and calculus or higher-level statistics
- Instrumental analysis, biochemistry, or genetics at the upper level

That is easily 20–30 credits of lab science, and it is sequential — general chemistry gates organic, which gates biochemistry. Starting the sequence late can cost you a semester or a year. Programs accredited by FEPAC (the Forensic Science Education Programs Accreditation Commission) are required to carry a substantial natural science core precisely for this reason.

One important, frequently missed point: a general criminal justice degree usually does **not** qualify you for bench work in a crime laboratory. Public crime labs commonly hire from chemistry, biology, and forensic science degrees with specific coursework requirements, and certification bodies in the field typically demand natural science credits that a standard criminal justice curriculum does not provide. If "CSI" is the goal, the science load is the major, not an add-on to it. If investigation, policy, courts, or corrections is the goal, the science-heavy path buys you very little.

## What actually counts as a "lab"

"Lab" is used loosely, and the way it is packaged determines both your credit count and your schedule.

**How labs are attached.** Some schools list a single 4-credit course that includes the lab. Others list a 3-credit lecture plus a separate 1-credit lab with a co-requisite rule, meaning you must register for both in the same term. If you take the lecture without the lab at a school that separates them, you may end up with a science course that does not satisfy the science-with-lab requirement.

**The credit-to-hour penalty.** A 1-credit lab typically meets for two to three hours a week. It is the worst credit-per-hour deal in your schedule, and lab sections are often scheduled in long afternoon blocks. For criminal justice students especially, that collides directly with internships, ride-alongs, field placements, and shift work. Schedule the lab in a term when your afternoons are free, not in the term you planned to intern.

**What usually qualifies.** Non-majors science courses with labs — physical geology, astronomy, environmental science, general physical science, some nutrition and anatomy courses — are standard, legitimate ways to satisfy the requirement, and they are typically less demanding than the majors' sequences. Choosing one of these over general chemistry is the single biggest lever on your science workload, and it is entirely within your control.

**Online and virtual labs.** Online programs satisfy lab requirements with at-home kits, simulation software, or intensive on-campus weekends. Acceptance varies, and the risk is mostly on transfer: a virtual lab that your current school accepts may not transfer cleanly elsewhere, and it is more likely to be questioned for pre-health pathways than for a political science or criminal justice degree. For these two majors specifically, it is usually low risk — but confirm before you pay for it.

| | Political science | Criminal justice |
|---|---|---|
| Usual degree | BA, sometimes BS | BA or BS |
| Science inside the major | Typically none | Typically none in the general track |
| Where science comes from | General education | General education |
| Typical lab requirement | One lab course | One lab course |
| Quantitative core | Methods, usually statistics | Methods and statistics |
| Where the science load explodes | A BS track | A forensic science concentration |
| Common language requirement | Frequent under a BA | Less common |

## Getting the real answer from your own catalog

Fifteen minutes of catalog reading beats any general article, including this one. Work through these in order:

1. **Pull the degree audit, not the brochure.** Run a what-if audit in your student system for both majors. It shows the actual boxes and which of your completed courses fill them.
2. **Separate the two lists.** Write down the major requirements and the general education requirements in separate columns. Confusing them is the root of nearly every mistake here.
3. **Find the exact words on the science line.** Look for "with laboratory," a credit minimum, and a course list. Note whether one or both science courses need the lab.
4. **Check BA versus BS.** If your school offers both for your major, compare them directly. This usually swings more credits than the choice of major does.
5. **Confirm the co-requisite rule.** Determine whether the lab is bundled into the course or registered separately.
6. **Verify any forensic survey course.** Ask your advisor in writing whether it counts as a natural science for general education, and whether its lab satisfies the lab requirement.
7. **Check your catalog year.** Requirements are tied to the year you entered or declared, and changing majors can move you to a newer catalog with different rules.
8. **If transferring, check articulation first.** Community college lab science is the cheapest way to clear this requirement, but confirm the specific course numbers transfer as lab science before you enroll.

## The bottom line

If your question is "which major has less science," political science and criminal justice are close to a tie, and both are usually light. The choice should turn on what you actually want to study and where you want to work — policy, government, law, and graduate study on one side; policing, courts, corrections, and investigation on the other, with law school open from either.

The science question only becomes decisive when a forensic laboratory career is on the table. At that point you are not choosing between two social science majors anymore. You are choosing whether to take a chemistry-heavy degree, and that decision is better made in your first year than your third.
